# Arcology construction and cross-section study
Working revision: September 25, 2026 — public summit and first massing direction confirmed

## Confirmed requirements

The author's current targets supersede the repository's 10-million-human baseline:

- **100 million human residents.**
- **100 million paired AI residents**, plus additional logistical and independent AI populations whose sizes and workloads remain undefined.
- **At least 750 sqft of private dwelling area per human**: a four-person family's home is approximately 3,000 sqft. Shared corridors, lobbies, civic facilities, parks, workplaces, and infrastructure are additional.
- Use the approved landing-page image as the visual reference: a broad terraced mass, flat rooftop park, and subsidiary stepped wings.
- **A large, flat public space at the summit**, open to everyone. The uppermost landscape is shared civic space: access to the city's most elevated and desirable location must not depend on wealth or residential address. This replaces the research's spire concept.
- **Variable main-mass tier count.** The former ten-tier assumption can change with structural, construction, transport, environmental, and space requirements.
- **Lead study: the housing-led case. Comparison: the more generous shared-city case.** Both must satisfy the same population, private-home minimum, and public-summit requirements.

The mile-high summit and 3.5-mile base remain legacy research assumptions, not newly confirmed constraints. No new site, wing dimensions, floor addresses, structural system, or operating budget is established by this study.

This is a construction concept and research brief. Its recommendations are proposals; its calculations are reproducible sensitivity studies, not completed engineering.

## What the landing-page image establishes

I inspected the live [landing-page artwork](https://lifewithai.ai/images/arcology/overview-storybook.webp). It shows a planted, broad, stepped central mass with a flat summit park, repeated facade divisions, terrace conservatories, three visible subsidiary ziggurats, and a river to the left. Repository art direction identifies six subsidiary ziggurats in total, three visible from this angle.

This can accommodate a useful architectural interpretation: many structural sections and inhabited districts sharing a terraced external form. The illustration does not establish that its visible ribs are sufficient buttresses, that the wings support the central mass, or that each illustrated window band equals a floor.

The relationship between the research and illustration is now:

1. Research uses square floor plates; the illustration reads as rounded or faceted, with projecting wings.
2. **Resolved design direction:** replace the spire with a large, flat public summit. Its area, landscape, amenities, structure, and access capacity remain to be designed; its public purpose is fixed.
3. **Updated design freedom:** the main mass is not constrained to ten tiers. The new model must allow tier count, tier heights, and setbacks to vary, and explicitly account for subsidiary wings and their overlaps.

Create the actual plan geometry first. Define whether “base width” measures the central body or the entire wing-to-wing envelope. Do not add satellite floor area if it is already inside a main-body footprint approximation. The river's location in the image does not establish available cooling water or a real site's flood conditions.

### The summit as a civic destination

The public summit is a design requirement with consequences for the whole city. Size its visitor capacity and access network together. Provide public routes from multiple parts of the Arcology, accessible connections, and alternatives when a route is unavailable. Distinguish universal entitlement to visit from the number of people who can be present simultaneously.

The summit study should show open landscape, public amenities, shelter, servicing, and access/refuge routes. Quantify planted-soil and water loads, wind exposure, drainage, and maintenance. Necessary plant and service areas should be integrated into the design while preserving the large public landscape.

Record rooftop public surface separately from enclosed floor area. Count accessible landscape once; do not turn the open air above it into extra floors or double-count the roof as both a park and an additional indoor story.

## Space requirement: homes first, then the city

The private residential minimum is:

**100 million × 750 sqft = 75 billion sqft = 6.968 billion m² of private homes.**

The legacy research allows 55.8 billion sqft of usable space across all uses, so it cannot meet the residential requirement even if every usable room became a home.

Two different efficiencies matter:
- **Residential efficiency:** private homes divided by the residential program, including shared residential space.
- **City efficiency:** usable program area divided by actual constructed gross floor area.

Neither is a measure of the fraction of the outer envelope filled by floors. A multi-story atrium has one accessible floor surface and substantial empty volume; it must not be counted as a usable floor on each story it spans.

| Scenario | Private homes / residential program | Residential / usable city | Usable city area | Gross floor area at 70% efficiency | Same-height square-tier base equivalent |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Reapply legacy allocation | 60% | 25% | 500 billion sqft | 714.3 billion sqft | 10.48 miles |
| Housing-led working case | 80% | 50% | 187.5 billion sqft | 267.9 billion sqft | 6.42 miles |
| More space for shared city functions | 75% | 40% | 250 billion sqft | 357.1 billion sqft | 7.41 miles |

All scenarios satisfy 750 sqft of private housing per human. Allocation ratios are assumptions, not demonstrated planning efficiencies. Equivalent widths scale the legacy square-tier geometry horizontally while holding height, floor count, and relative taper constant. They are not measurements of the illustration or proposed final dimensions. New courtyard voids, large openings, facade geometry, and subsidiary wings will change them.

**Confirmed study direction:** use the housing-led case for the first massing study and retain the more generous shared-city case as a comparison. The working case has 3.36 times the legacy gross floor area and an equivalent footprint of about 106.6 km². These comparisons describe the old square-tier approximation, not a commitment to ten tiers or that footprint. Recalculate dimensions from the new plan and void accounting.

A separate budget should specify homes, shared residential spaces, schools and healthcare, commerce, parks, transit, utilities, compute, and reserve. Assign every square meter once. The legacy 10% compute-area allowance is particularly worth replacing with rack, cooling, power-room, maintenance, and redundancy requirements; compute demand and floor demand need not grow together.

For the new study, compute is therefore a demand-derived program item rather than a fixed 10% allocation. Its resulting area must fit within the city budget or trigger an explicit revision; it is not an uncounted addition.

The comparison adds **62.5 billion sqft of usable program**: 6.25 billion more shared residential space and 56.25 billion more for other city functions. Private homes remain the same size. At the same 70% city efficiency, constructed area rises by one-third. These are program allowances; schools, workplaces, utilities, indoor parks, compute, and reserve still need individual quantities.

Two assumptions change between these cases: residential efficiency falls from 80% to 75%, and residential share falls from 50% to 40%. The massing study should also vary them separately so that larger civic provision is distinguishable from additional residential circulation and amenities.

The test district's share of total city program is 37.5 million usable sqft in the housing-led case and 50 million in the comparison. Some of that share will be delivered in citywide facilities elsewhere; every district need not duplicate every specialist facility.

## Construction system to investigate

My recommendation is a **terraced group of structural sections, with many operationally isolatable districts inside them**.

This is a middle-scale organization:
- Whole-city systems are shared where scale helps.
- Structural sections have deliberate load paths and movement boundaries.
- Districts can isolate faults and continue essential services.
- Individual homes and interior spaces use repeatable, maintainable components.

Do not equate visual tiers with independent buildings stacked on one another. Tier count and geometry are design variables; every upper tier still relies on supports below. Transferring a load sideways does not erase that load or reset accumulated support movement.

### Three structural alternatives

| Alternative | Benefit | Main unresolved cost |
|---|---|---|
| One continuous structural mass | Potential stiffness and material sharing | Enormous settlement, thermal-movement, phasing, and failure-propagation problem |
| Clustered vertical structural sections under a common stepped form | Clearer foundations, sequencing, movement boundaries, and utility isolation | Tallest sections still need exceptional lateral stability; connecting them may create structural dependence |
| Large shared platforms carrying smaller neighborhood buildings | Legible “cities on decks” for stories and replacement of some infill | Transfer decks become extremely heavy, important shared supports; independent neighborhoods do not imply independent structure |

**Start with the clustered-sections alternative.** Use aligned cores, columns, and braced/composite frames to carry upper loads down to foundations. Treat giant transfer decks as special cases that must earn their cost, not the default for every tier.

SOM's description of Burj Khalifa specifically emphasizes aligning upper columns with lower walls to maintain smooth load paths through setbacks. This is a useful principle, not evidence that the Arcology's size is feasible. [SOM structural description](https://www.som.com/projects/burj-khalifa/).

For each proposed section, decide whether neighboring connections are:
- structurally coupled and essential for stability;
- movement-tolerant pedestrian/transit links; or
- utility connections with isolation at their ends.

A structurally necessary brace cannot simultaneously be treated as a disconnectable bridge. Subsidiary wings can be independently phased only if the central mass does not rely on their unfinished structure.

The existing materials entry says tier transfers reset shortening accumulation. That needs correction: upper elevations still reflect movement and shortening of supports below. Construction sequence, creep, shrinkage, thermal movement, and differential settlement must be calculated together. Source: [materials-at-scale.md](https://github.com/YourLifewithAI/Lifewithai/blob/ae7a9df53c8cecbb798f5ebf8d9c1e5b97eb303c/content/knowledge/structural-engineering/materials/materials-at-scale.md).

### Material strategy

Use conventional, inspectable systems as the initial quantity-model baseline:
- Reinforced or composite cores, walls, columns, and foundations sized from load paths.
- Repetitive floor systems evaluated for span, vibration, fire performance, acoustic performance, and mass.
- Prefabricated service assemblies, facade panels, and interior fit-out.
- High-strength concrete or steel only where analysis shows a useful reduction in mass or congestion.

Keep UHPC, fiber reinforcement, self-healing systems, and autonomous placement as alternatives with explicit evidence requirements. Do not make universal availability, rapid placement, extraordinary cost savings, or century-scale durability prerequisites hidden inside the baseline. Robot productivity must be expressed as installed quantities per day, availability, supervision, maintenance, and rejection/rework rates.

## Material and construction-energy consequences

Even the legacy area is inconsistent with research material totals. In the housing-led case, constructed gross area is approximately **24.885 billion m²**.

A simple floor-material sensitivity—**150–250 mm equivalent concrete thickness across that area**—produces:
- **3.73–6.22 billion m³ of concrete-equivalent floor material**.
- Approximately **8.96–14.93 billion tonnes**, if represented by concrete at 2.4 t/m³.
- **124–207 million m³ annually over 30 years**, before cores, columns, foundations, or other building elements.

This is not a slab specification, minimum material requirement, or bill of quantities. A different floor system changes it. It demonstrates that material quantities must be linked to actual floor area; the repository's 50–200 million m³ project figures cannot simply be carried forward.

Construction energy needs three separate accounts:
1. **Supply-chain energy:** cement/clinker, steel, aggregates, glass, prefabrication, and transport.
2. **Site energy:** excavation, processing, cranes, hoists, pumps, temporary ventilation, and construction facilities.
3. **Occupied-phase energy:** completed districts operating while later sections are built.

For one transparent example, assume 0.30 tonnes cement per m³ of the floor proxy, a 0.71 clinker ratio, and 3.6 GJ of kiln heat per tonne of clinker. The latter two are historical IEA reference values, not selected future material specifications. This gives approximately **795–1,325 TWh of kiln thermal energy** across construction for those floors alone. It excludes electricity, steel, foundations, and other supply-chain work, and is not all drawn through the Arcology's site grid. [IEA cement analysis](https://www.iea.org/reports/cement-3).

Cost has the same scope problem. Applying the repository's own illustrative $150/sqft rate to 267.9 billion gross sqft gives about **$40 trillion**. That is sensitivity arithmetic, not an independent estimate, but it rules out retaining the $800 billion narrative without a radically different quantity/cost model.

## Operating energy and human flows

The existing 9.5 GW budget is not a valid budget for the clarified population and residential program. It already contains conflicting loads and arithmetic at the smaller population.

A useful area-led sensitivity for the housing-led case is to assume 75% of usable city area is conditioned non-compute space: **13.064 billion m²**.

| Hypothetical building electricity intensity | Annual building electricity | Average power |
|---|---:|---:|
| 50 kWh/m²/year | 653 TWh | 74.6 GW |
| 100 kWh/m²/year | 1,306 TWh | 149.1 GW |
| 150 kWh/m²/year | 1,960 TWh | 223.7 GW |

These intensities are exploration inputs, not verified benchmarks or predictions. The cases exclude AI IT and other separately modeled process loads. They show how strongly energy depends on the amount and treatment of internal space. Do not add a per-person building-energy estimate to these area-based totals: that would double-count overlapping demand.

At the repository's other assumptions:
- 200 L/person/day gives **20 billion liters/day** of domestic demand.
- Recovering 95% of that stream leaves **1 billion liters/day** of makeup, before cooling and industrial/agricultural demands.
- 2,000 kcal/person/day gives **200 billion kcal/day**, before distribution losses.
- 100 W/person gives **10 GW of metabolic heat**.

These are arithmetic scalings, not proof that the recovery rate, diet, or cooling plan works. The district model must integrate electricity, heat, water, and food rather than treating them as separate percentage allocations.

### AI population and compute

The legacy 26,800 racks × 72 GPUs gives about 1.93 million GPUs: approximately **52 paired AI per GPU**, before additional residents and logistics. That is an accounting ratio, not a performance result.

Define at least:
- paired-resident interaction, background activity, memory, and continuity;
- independent AI workloads and minimum service guarantees;
- logistical/control workloads, including local operation during network loss;
- commercial training/inference that can be scheduled or reduced.

Specify concurrent activity, model sizes, throughput, memory, latency, state replication, and failure tolerance before sizing the fleet. Shared model weights may change memory requirements, but do not remove resident-state or inference requirements.

Treat continuity for AI residents as an explicit design requirement in the fictional city. Do not assume “load shedding” can indiscriminately stop residents' processes. Define priorities and protected capacity separately from discretionary commercial workloads.

### Heat is an external dependency

Heat reuse is helpful when a suitable demand exists, but internal heat exchange cannot dispose of the city's net heat indefinitely. Texas summer conditions make heat rejection a first-order site and architecture question.

Keep human heating demand, IT heat, electric chiller demand, and power-plant rejection in separate accounts. The legacy 6.175 GW of IT alone would evaporate approximately **218 million liters/day** if all its heat were removed by evaporation at 2.45 MJ/kg, before blowdown and other facility loads. That is a sensitivity example; dry/hybrid cooling and heat reuse change the water requirement.

Bulk generation and heat-rejection facilities should be represented in the regional site section, rather than hidden under residential floors or assumed to disappear into the illustrated river. The DOE treats IT, cooling, electrical systems, and heat recovery as interdependent design categories. [DOE data-center design guide](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/femp/articles/best-practices-guide-energy-efficient-data-center-design).

## What “independent functional units” should mean

Independence is specific to a service, failure, and duration. An upper-floor district can have isolated air, water, electricity, and data while remaining structurally dependent on many floors below.

Use a test district of **20,000 humans plus 20,000 paired AI**, with its additional local AI demand recorded separately. That implies 5,000 districts citywide. This population is a modeling convenience, not an adopted neighborhood size or fire compartment.

Each test district needs **15 million sqft of private homes**. Under the housing-led allocation, it corresponds to about **37.5 million sqft of usable city program** and **53.6 million sqft gross**. It is a large district containing many smaller blocks; it is not one apartment tower or one fire zone.

| System | District capability to investigate | Continuing shared dependence |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | Defined section, alternate paths within its design, monitored joints | Foundations and supporting structure; possibly coupled lateral system |
| Electricity | Local protection, critical-load backup, controlled disconnection | Regional generation, replenishment, independent supply routes |
| Air and cooling | Separate smoke/air zones, local controls, emergency cooling strategy | Shared heat rejection unless specifically duplicated |
| Water | Pressure zone, isolation valves, local storage, quality monitoring | Makeup source and bulk treatment |
| Wastewater | Isolation, local pumping and temporary storage or treatment | Downstream treatment and residual disposal |
| Food | Local stocks, distribution, selected fresh production | Staple imports, agricultural inputs, replenishment |
| Human and AI services | Local healthcare access, resident continuity, local control | Specialist care, replicated AI state and compute, shared networks |
| Transport | Protected local routes and access to nearby refuge | Interdistrict routes and safe destinations; capacity under degraded operation |

A district is not resilient if its two power feeders share one vulnerable shaft, or if its backup compute depends on the same failed cooling loop. Map physical routes as well as logical networks.

Use explicit outage exercises: lost feeder, severed pipe, failed district chiller, unavailable transit link, contaminated water zone, and disconnected city network. For each, state maintained services, load reductions, evacuation/refuge implications, and time until replenishment is necessary.

Consider a **72-hour essential-service scenario** for comparison, not a claim of autonomy or a validated requirement. Different systems may require different endurance targets. Determine the actual inventories and cooling required, including AI resident continuity.

Structural failure containment also needs real analysis. Isolation joints alone do not prove that failure cannot propagate, and tightly connected frames can gain robustness from alternate load paths. [NIST guidance on progressive collapse](https://www.nist.gov/publications/best-practices-reducing-potential-progressive-collapse-buildings).

## Where functions should go

These are placement proposals for the first section, not fixed floor assignments:

- **Terraces, perimeter, and selected internal courtyards:** homes, schools, local services, and parks with meaningful daylight and outdoor access.
- **Intermediate inhabited bands:** mixed neighborhoods, workplaces, clinics, civic uses, and distributed refuge. Avoid separating all housing from all employment vertically.
- **Service corridors and plant rooms alongside districts:** accessible utility distribution, pressure breaks, fire separation, waste collection, and maintenance access. A district utility center must not become its single failure point.
- **Distributed transit intersections:** express/local passenger interchange, separate freight routes, and access to multiple districts. Do not send all city movement through one central shaft.
- **Lower and peripheral service districts:** major freight handling, workshops, heavy processing, and larger water-treatment facilities, located by loads, flood conditions, nuisance, and access.
- **Several compute campuses or halls:** lower/peripheral locations with direct power and heat-rejection access, plus local edge facilities. Avoid concentrating every AI resident's critical infrastructure beneath one flood- or foundation-dependent zone.
- **Regional infrastructure outside the inhabited mass:** bulk generation, cooling systems, reservoirs and treatment where appropriate, agriculture, material production, and regional transport.
- **Summit:** provide the confirmed large, flat public landscape, open to everyone, with distributed public access and explicit visitor capacity. Budget soil, water, shelter, servicing, wind exposure, and supporting loads.
- **Upper terraces:** connect public landscapes and everyday destinations to the summit access network; detailed uses remain part of the program study.

Daylight must be designed into the plan. A windowed perimeter does not make kilometers of interior floor plate naturally lit. Deep courts and openings consume floor area and may affect smoke, wind, structure, and circulation. Fully enclosed atria require their own lighting and thermal budgets.

## Construction sequence to test

1. Establish real site geology, hydrology, flood exposure, transport access, and regional resource availability.
2. Build the initial industrial/logistics and utility capacity needed for a finite first occupied phase.
3. Construct a first structural section or subsidiary wing with complete access, utilities, and life-safety provisions.
4. Prove operation with residents and measured human/AI demand before repeating the district.
5. Add neighboring sections and utility loops in phases with explicit final-load provisions.
6. Extend the taller central sections where their foundations, supports, and construction access are already accounted for.
7. Connect public routes and landscape as each section becomes independently occupiable.

Do not assume early occupants can live safely beneath unrestricted heavy construction. Prefer laterally separated work fronts where possible, with project-specific protection and alternate access for unavoidable vertical overlaps. Do not require an unfinished wing or future utility plant for a currently occupied district's essential operation.

Each phase needs a completion state that functions if the next phase is delayed indefinitely. This is more meaningful than a calendar promising the entire city in 20–30 years.

## Cross-sections to develop next

Prepare coordinated schematic drawings before finished storybook cutaways:

1. **Regional site section:** city footprint, foundations, nearby terrain and water, generation, heat rejection, freight, and outside food/material dependencies.
2. **Overall structural section:** central mass plus a subsidiary wing, continuous supports, structural/movement boundaries, floor-bearing zones, large voids, and summit park.
3. **Typical district section and matching plan:** homes, local services, daylight courts, terraces, plant access, passenger/freight separation, and smaller safety compartments.
4. **Utility interface section:** two neighboring districts, showing what can isolate, what remains shared, and where backup services actually connect.
5. **Construction-stage section:** one occupied section, one under construction, and one future section, including temporary utilities and protected access.

A section alone cannot establish household area or long-distance connectivity; pair it with a plan and an area schedule. Trace three things through every drawing: **weight to ground, people to destinations/refuge, and heat to the environment**. Also trace water, power, data, and materials across unit boundaries.

Use different visual conventions for confirmed requirements, proposed architecture, and unresolved components. Keep dimensions and component sizes provisional until supported by the shared model.

### Next deliverable: massing study 01

Develop the whole-city envelope and a representative district together, in this order:

1. **Program ledger.** Carry forward the two case totals and divide their nonresidential allowances among actual uses. Separate indoor usable area, constructed gross area, outdoor public surface, and unbuilt void volume. Size compute from a workload/equipment scenario; keep unknown demand explicit.
2. **Adjustable whole-city geometry.** Model the central mass and subsidiary wings as actual plan shapes with overlap removal. Vary tier count, height, setback, summit size, wing dimensions, and courtyards. Report enclosed floor area after void deductions against each case's required gross area. Keep the existing width equivalents only as historical comparison values.
3. **Public-summit access study.** Test ordinary and busy-day visitor scenarios, journey paths, capacity, accessible routes, and partial route outages. Use these results to reserve access and service space through the lower city.
4. **Representative district plan and section.** Start with the provisional 20,000-human/20,000-paired-AI district, containing multiple smaller blocks. Show the homes, shared facilities, daylight/terrace relationships, supporting structure, utilities, and connections. Separate its local facilities from its allocation of citywide facilities.
5. **Integrated comparison.** For both cases, reconcile area, movement, preliminary material quantities, construction sequence, and utility loads. Identify changes needed in the envelope or program. Draw load paths and utility boundaries without presenting uncalculated member sizes as settled engineering.

The output should be a paired set of dimensioned massing diagrams, a representative district plan/section, and one reconciled area-and-dependency schedule. Choose the next architectural direction from those results before producing finished explainer illustrations.
